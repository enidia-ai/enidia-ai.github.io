Why not a general legal AI

Why not a general legal AI for checklists and forms?

General assistants are built for a different shape of work.

The large legal-AI platforms are good products for open-ended work: research a question, summarise a document, draft a first pass, chat with a data room. That is a different job from filling a fixed set of questions and standing behind the result, and the difference is structural, not a matter of quality.

The problem

By hand it takes days. With a general AI, you still cannot sign without checking everything.

Friday afternoon. A partner hands an associate a fund's partnership agreement and the firm's checklist: fill this in by Monday. The agreement runs well over a hundred pages; the checklist asks the usual questions about the management fee, the key person clause, transfers, most favoured nation.

Option one: read it all

The associate's weekend goes into the agreement. For a large one, that is days of work, and the next fund brings the same checklist.

Option two: ask a general AI

It fills in the checklist in minutes. But nobody hands the partner answers they have not checked, so the associate goes back to the agreement to confirm each one. By the time the last answer is checked, the work has taken almost as long as reading the agreement in the first place. Meanwhile the client's document sits in a vendor's cloud.

Monday morning, question 1

Where is it written?

Question 2

What did the AI guess?

Question 3

What did it miss?

Neither option gives a quick answer. One costs days of reading; the other, days of checking. Replace the agreement with any pile of documents, a data room, a prospectus, a company's articles and contracts, and it is the same process with the same problems.

The difference

Six structural reasons

AI is useful to a lawyer only if checking its work is faster than doing the work. These are the reasons a general assistant fails that test on a checklist, whatever its quality.

They answer. They do not report coverage.

A general assistant is built to produce an answer to whatever you ask. Ask it fifty questions and you get fifty answers, including for the questions your documents never addressed. The thing you actually need to know on a filing is which answers the documents support, and there is nothing in a chat transcript that tells you.

A missing fact comes back as a confident sentence.

Silence is the expensive failure in this task, and it is invisible in a generative tool: the gap looks exactly like an answer. Our whole design is that the gap is a visible, countable state you have to sign off, not a blank you might never notice.

A citation written by the AI is itself generated.

So it must be checked too. A page reference next to a paraphrase is not something a reviewer can check without re-reading the document, and to check one answer the lawyer reopens the two hundred pages. The time saved is spent again. Every answer here opens on the passage it rests on, so checking it is reading one quote.

They improvise each time.

A chat assistant or general agent chooses its own steps on every run, so the same question can get different answers on Monday and Friday, and the firm's quality depends on who typed the prompt. A fixed workflow runs the same steps on every item, every document and every year, which is what a compliance team files and an auditor checks.

Their pricing punishes exactly this workload.

A form of fifty questions against a hundred-page document is the most token-hungry shape of legal work there is. On a per-seat-plus-metered-usage model, the task that most needs automating is the one that costs most to automate, so firms ration it. Ours does not meter, because it runs on a machine you own.

They need the largest cloud models, so the documents go to the model.

A general assistant reads a whole document at once, and with a context of a million tokens nobody knows which pages it used and which it skipped. It is reliable only on the largest models, which live in a cloud. A fixed pipeline asks narrow questions with the evidence in front of the model, lists the pages it searched and the pages that gave evidence, and runs on small open models on one server inside the firm.

The honest version: if you want one tool for everything, buy one of theirs. If you have checklists, forms or questionnaires to fill and someone has to be able to defend the result, that is the job we built for, and we do it better, cheaper and more safely than a general tool can.

The standard

The question every answer must survive: "Where did you take this from?"

The lawyer who signs stays responsible, whoever drafted the answer. A general assistant gives a fluent answer and no way to check it quickly. Enidia is built to the standard a partner already applies to an associate.

How a senior lawyer already works

A partner delegates the reading to a junior associate and checks the result. The check is quick for one reason: the partner can always ask "Where did you take this from?" and the associate opens the page. Enidia answers the same way, with the page and the quoted passage behind every answer. The check is reading the answer next to its quote.

Why the standard is not optional

Professional rules keep the lawyer responsible for what goes out. The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 of 29 July 2024 says lawyers cannot abdicate their professional judgment to generative AI. And AI invents: AI-invented material has been found in 2,293 court decisions, according to the AI Hallucination Cases database kept by Damien Charlotin, as of 14 November 2026.

Questions

General legal AI, in plain answers

Can a general legal AI fill a checklist?

It will give fifty answers to fifty questions, including for the questions your documents never addressed. What it does not tell you is which answers the documents support.

What happens to a missing fact in a generative tool?

It comes back as a confident sentence. The gap looks exactly like an answer, which is why silence is the expensive failure in this task.

Is a citation enough to verify an answer?

Not on its own. A citation written by the AI is itself generated, so it must be checked too, and a page reference next to a paraphrase cannot be checked without re-reading the document. The passage an answer rests on has to open on its page.

Why is checking a general AI's answer slow?

Because the lawyer who signs stays responsible, so every answer has to be confirmed against the document. A chat assistant gives a fluent answer and no quick way to check it: the citation is generated, and with a context of a million tokens nobody knows which pages the model read. By the time the last answer is checked, the work has taken almost as long as reading the document in the first place.

Does a chat assistant give the same answer twice?

Not reliably. It chooses its own steps each time, so the same question can get different answers on Monday and Friday, and the firm's quality depends on who typed the prompt. A fixed pipeline runs the same steps on every item and every document, so the result can be repeated and defended.

Should we buy a general tool instead?

If you want one tool for everything, yes. If you have checklists, forms or questionnaires to fill and someone has to be able to defend the result, that is the job Enidia is built for.

Why does metered pricing matter for this task?

A form of fifty questions against a hundred-page document is the most token-hungry shape of legal work there is. Under metered pricing, the task that most needs automating costs most to automate, so firms ration it.
