On-premise legal AI

On-premise legal AI: document review that runs on hardware you own

The work stays in the building. So does the bill.

On-premise legal AI runs the model on a machine inside your firm's network instead of in a vendor's cloud. Documents are read where they already sit, so nothing is sent to a third-party model and there is no transfer to assess. Enidia's AssociAte works this way in production: one workstation of about €5,000, a licence, and no metered usage on top.

Deploy. Where the work happens, and what never leaves the building.

What changes

Who can see your documents, and what a heavy month costs

On-premise is the part of this that competitors cannot copy without rebuilding their business, and it changes two things at once: who can see your documents, and what a heavy month costs you.

Privilege stays intact

Nothing is sent to a third-party model. There is no processor to add to your record, no sub-processor list to chase, and no transfer to assess, because there is no transfer.

The documents you cannot send anywhere

Matters under embargo, client data with contractual bans on external processing, regulated material. These are exactly the documents with the most manual work in them, and the only ones a cloud tool can never touch.

Fixed cost, unlimited runs

The machine costs what it costs. Running a fifty-question form over three hundred documents costs the same as running it over three, so nobody has to ask permission before using it.

A machine, not a data centre

It runs perfectly well on hardware costing around €5,000. That is one workstation under a desk, which puts it within reach of a three-partner boutique and a single fund administrator, not only of firms with an infrastructure budget.

Scaling costs about €5,000

Double the people using it and you have not doubled a bill, because there is no per-seat bill. If the queue gets long, you add a second machine. That is the whole upgrade path, and you own what you bought.

It still works when the vendor changes

Price rises, model deprecations, an outage, an acquisition. Software on your own hardware keeps doing the same thing tomorrow as it did today.

Your corpus is searchable

Because the system lives where your documents live, it can work across your own back catalogue, the firm's own precedents, not just the file in front of you.

An artefact you can hand over

Every run produces a certificate: each element, marked proven, uncertain or not found, with its verbatim source and its locator. It goes into the matter file, to the client, or in front of a regulator.

Where the documents are

Inside the building, everything. Outside it, nothing.

The supported solution is one server inside your network, running open models and Enidia's workflows. The left column is what stays in the building. The right column is what never happens.

Inside the firm's building

What stays here

Client documents never leave the firm. No US cloud, so no exposure to the CLOUD Act, under which US authorities can demand data from a US provider wherever it is stored.

Open models, run locally. Any open model, Mistral included, on the firm's own hardware. When a better one appears, you swap it. No lock-in to one lab.

The firm's knowledge stays its own. Templates, corrections and completed checklists are stored on the firm's server and serve the firm alone.

Purpose limitation, by construction. Each run uses only the documents chosen for it, and nothing is reused elsewhere. That is the GDPR's purpose limitation built in, not promised.

Outside the building

What never happens

No token provider. No account with an AI lab, no documents sent to one, no per-answer bill.

No vendor cloud. No document is processed outside the firm's network, not even by Enidia.

No training on your data. Nothing the firm does feeds a shared model, and nothing it teaches the tool reaches another firm.

No surprise in the engagement letter. When a client asks where their documents are, the firm can answer in one word: here.

CLOUD Act: the US Clarifying Lawful Overseas Use of Data Act, 2018. The online demo and the free trial run in our hosted environment on public or shareable documents only; confidential material waits for the machine.

Why one server is enough

Small open models, because the workflow asks small questions

A chat assistant reads a whole document at once and needs the largest cloud models to be reliable, so the documents go to the model. Enidia runs a checklist as a fixed pipeline: for each item it finds the passages, answers, then checks the answer against its passage. The model only ever sees a narrow question with the evidence in front of it.

Reliable on one machine

Narrow questions with the evidence attached are what small open models answer well. That is what lets one server in the firm do the work, without the largest models and without the cloud they live in.

You choose the model

Mistral and other open models run today. The firm picks the model, and swaps it when a better one appears. No vendor decides for you, and no retirement notice changes your work.

Same steps every time

The same steps on every item, every document, every year, with verification built in. The result can be repeated, checked and shown to a partner, an auditor or the regulator, field by field.

Questions

On-premise legal AI, in plain answers

What hardware does on-premise legal AI need?

One workstation. It runs perfectly well on hardware costing around €5,000, which puts it within reach of a three-partner boutique and a single fund administrator, not only of firms with an infrastructure budget.

Do our documents ever leave the building?

Not in production. The documents are read on a machine inside your firm and nothing is sent to a third-party model. The free trial is different: it runs in our cloud on a document you are free to share, and confidential material waits for the machine.

What does it cost when more people use it?

Nothing more. There is no per-seat bill, so twice the users runs on the same machine. If the queue gets long, you add a second machine of about €5,000 and own it.

What happens if a model provider changes its prices or retires a model?

Nothing changes for you. Software on your own hardware keeps doing the same thing tomorrow as it did today, whatever happens to price lists, models or vendors.

Can it work across our own back catalogue?

Yes. Because the system lives where your documents live, it can work across the firm's own precedents, not just the file in front of you.

Does the CLOUD Act reach our documents?

Not when the tool runs on your own server. The CLOUD Act lets US authorities demand data from a US provider wherever it is stored. With no US cloud and no AI lab involved, there is no provider to serve the demand on.

Which AI model does it use, and can we change it?

Any open model, Mistral included, running on the firm's own hardware. The workflow asks the model narrow questions with the evidence in front of it, so a small open model is enough, and you swap it when a better one appears.
