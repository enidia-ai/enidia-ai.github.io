Neither of these breaches was a cloud failure. That is not the reassuring part.

The Enidia AI team
November 12, 2026

Two proposed class actions were filed on 4 November against Baker McKenzie and Squire Patton Boggs over cyberattacks. On the reporting so far, neither incident was a failure of a cloud provider, and a tool like ours would not have prevented either. What the two complaints do show is where the liability lands, and that raises a question about law firm data breaches and client confidentiality: how many copies of a client’s documents exist outside the firm’s control?

On 6 November the ABA Journal, following Reuters, reported two proposed class actions filed two days earlier in federal court: one against Baker McKenzie in the Northern District of Illinois, one against Squire Patton Boggs in the Northern District of Ohio. In both, the plaintiffs allege that cyberattacks at the firm exposed their personal information. The allegations are unproven and neither class has been certified. Neither firm responded to Reuters’ request for comment.

The firms’ own descriptions of what happened are worth reading carefully, because they do not say what a vendor like us would find convenient.

According to Reuters, Baker McKenzie said a cybercriminal in August had “socially engineered two of our users” to obtain a limited number of files, and that “there was no independent malicious access to our system.” Squire Patton Boggs, disclosing its incident in October, described “an unauthorized third party obtaining a limited set of information from the firm.”

Read plainly: as far as the public reporting goes, neither of these was a cloud provider being breached. In one case, two people were deceived. No arrangement of infrastructure stops that, and anyone selling you on premise software as the answer to social engineering is selling you something.

The vendor does not get sued. The firm does.

Look instead at who the defendants are: the firms.

That is the pattern worth taking seriously, and it does not depend on how the attacker got in. When a client’s information is exposed, the claim, the regulator’s questions and the client’s phone call all arrive at the firm whose name is on the engagement letter. The firm cannot delegate that. It can only delegate the data.

Which raises the question we think every firm should be able to answer on demand: how many copies of a given client file exist right now, and in whose systems?

For most firms the honest answer has been growing for ten years. The document management system. Email, several mailboxes deep. A sharing platform. An e-discovery vendor. A translation service. And now, increasingly, an AI tool that has to send the document somewhere in order to read it.

Every one of those is a set of credentials that can be phished, a staff list that can be socially engineered, and often a contract in which someone else’s liability is capped at a year of fees. The firm’s responsibility to its client is not capped in the same way.

Where on premise legal AI sits in that sentence

In production, Enidia reads the firm’s documents on the firm’s own hardware, inside the firm’s own network. It does not create a copy of a client file in our systems, because we do not have one. There are no credentials to our cloud, because a production deployment does not use one. If you are counting the places a client’s prospectus or due diligence file exists tonight, we add zero to the count. (Our free trial does run in our cloud, which is why it is meant for documents you could share anyway.)

That is a narrow claim and we want to keep it narrow. It is not a claim that your firm cannot be breached. It is a claim about one specific thing: adopting AI across a practice does not have to mean adding another external place where client documents live.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Because the system runs inside the firm, the logs are the firm’s own. If you do have to tell a client what was accessed and when, you are reading your own records rather than waiting on a vendor’s incident response team and their lawyers.

What this has to do with agents

Our CTO, Aleksander Smywiński-Pohl, made a related point in his interview on 4 November. An autonomous agent without the full context can take an action that looks harmless, like sending an email with a question to someone in the firm, and still breach an NDA or GDPR because of what the question reveals.

Put that next to the Baker McKenzie description. Social engineering works on people who are helpful and busy. Agents are helpful and never busy. As firms give agents the ability to send, fetch and request, the attack surface stops being the number of employees and becomes the number of actions a system is permitted to take.

That is why our agents answer questions and produce a certificate rather than going off and doing things: Found, Inferred or Absent, each with its verbatim source and its locator. The system reads and reports. It does not act on the firm’s behalf.

For firms in Luxembourg

Professional secrecy does not get softer because a document was processed by software, and the firm remains the controller for the personal data it holds.

For the fund administrators and management companies we work with, there is a further layer. Under DORA, regulated financial entities now maintain a register of their ICT third party arrangements. Every external system touching client material is an entry in that register and a question in somebody’s audit. A system with no external component is a short answer to a long questionnaire. That is not the most inspiring reason to choose a tool. It may be the most practical one.

What we are not saying

We are not saying Baker McKenzie or Squire Patton Boggs did anything wrong. The allegations are untested and both firms describe limited incidents.

We are not saying on premise is safer in the abstract. A badly run server inside your building is worse than a well run cloud. On premise moves the responsibility to you, which is the point, and only an advantage if you want it.

And we are not a security product. We are an answering system that happens not to need your documents to leave. For the firms we talk to in Luxembourg, that turns out to be the feature that gets the meeting.
