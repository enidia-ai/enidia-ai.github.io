Analysis · AI budgets

The real limit on the AI boom is the finance department

The Enidia AI team
November 5, 2026

Large companies are capping how much AI each employee may use. Apple has cut its engineers’ daily allowance, Accenture has put a monthly cap per employee, Uber spent a year of budget in four months, and Meta has introduced token budgets. For work that is heavy on documents, like filling regulatory forms, metered AI pricing makes the most useful task the most expensive one. That is the problem Enidia AI was built around.

What is happening to corporate AI budgets

A thread by Ricardo (@Ric_RTP), published this week, collects what several companies have done, citing Bloomberg, 404 Media and company filings.

Apple reportedly reduced some engineers’ daily AI spending cap from 300 to 200 dollars, with 100 dollars under consideration for some teams, while steering work towards cheaper models and models that run on the device. Accenture capped one assistant at 29 dollars a month per employee from 1 October, after some staff had reportedly been spending up to 1,000 dollars a month. Uber is described as having used its entire 2026 AI budget in about four months, then capping coding tools at 1,500 dollars a month each. Microsoft has trimmed allowances, and Meta, which once ranked staff on an internal leaderboard by tokens consumed, now sets token budgets.

Set against that, Nvidia’s Jensen Huang said in March that he would be alarmed if an engineer paid 500,000 dollars used less than 250,000 dollars of AI tokens a year.

We have not verified each figure ourselves. They come from the reporting the thread cites. The direction, however, is not in doubt: the people paying the bill are writing rules that push spending per person down.

Why this matters more for legal and regulatory work

A fifty question checklist run against a hundred page prospectus is one of the most token-hungry shapes of professional work there is. On a per seat plus metered usage model, that makes the task which most needs automating the one that costs most to automate. Teams then learn not to run the big job twice, and a tool that is rationed is a tool that is not used.

A missed filing deadline or a form sent back by the regulator costs more than the licence. Nobody should be deciding whether to re-run a check on an amended document because of a token budget.

More and more companies are cutting internal AI spending and using local AI to do part of the work. Running smaller models on your own hardware is no longer the cautious option. It is becoming the affordable one.

What we do about the cost problem

Enidia runs on hardware the firm owns, and the licence is a flat fee. There is no metered usage, so running a fifty question form over three hundred documents costs the same as running it over three, and nobody has to ask permission before using it. The machine costs what it costs.

That is not a reaction to this week’s news. It is the architecture that came out of the research, and the news is the market arriving at the same conclusion.
