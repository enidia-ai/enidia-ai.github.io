Billing for AI time: what the Tenth Circuit and the New York bar said

The Enidia AI team
November 5, 2026

On 4 November 2026 a US federal appeals court upheld a 115,000 dollar legal fee award even though the lawyers had used artificial intelligence. One day earlier, the New York State Bar Association adopted guidance on how AI costs may be charged to clients. Together they show that AI does not reduce a bill automatically, because the lawyer still has to verify the work, and verification is work. What neither answers is how a firm proves that the verification happened.

What did the Tenth Circuit decide about AI and fee awards?

On 4 November 2026 the ABA Journal reported, from Reuters, that the 10th US Circuit Court of Appeals upheld a 115,000 dollar fee award to defence lawyers at Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck. The underlying case was a trade secrets dispute brought by Flux Drive, a maker of motor efficiency equipment, against a Chinese manufacturer.

The losing side argued that the defence had used AI and that the bill should therefore have been smaller. A unanimous three judge panel disagreed. Judge Carolyn McHugh wrote that “Plaintiffs have not shown that defense counsel’s use of AI rendered the hours that they actually expended unreasonable.”

According to the report, the judge accepted that AI and similar research tools can make lawyers more efficient, while stressing that lawyers must still verify the authorities and arguments themselves. The defence said it had checked every piece of AI assisted research, and that the other side had pointed to no specific inflated entry or unreasonable charge.

Christopher Ottele, the Brownstein Hyatt partner who acted for the defendants, told Reuters: “This decision confirms that the use of AI is not a substitute for the judgment and analysis that attorneys still must exercise in every filing with the court.”

What did the New York State Bar Association say about billing for AI?

On 3 November 2026 the ABA Journal reported, from Law360, that the New York State Bar Association had adopted a report from its Committee on Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies.

On billing, the report treats the cost of buying and maintaining AI software as an overhead expense. It may be passed to a client as a disbursement only when it is tied to a specific matter and the basis for the charge has been disclosed. Lawyers generally do not have to tell clients about AI used for administrative work such as word processing, spell checking, calendaring or timekeeping, provided it does not affect fees.

Taa Grays, the association’s president, said in a statement: “Generative AI is indelibly changing the practice of law. As part of our mission to be the leading voice for the legal profession, we are providing clear guidelines on the use of AI tools as a resource, so attorneys can use AI responsibly and ethically in their practices.”

One honest note. The headline of that second report says the bar is telling lawyers not to bill for time saved by AI. The substance, as described, is narrower: overhead, disbursements and disclosure. We have read the account, not the report itself, so treat the headline as the journalist’s framing rather than as a rule.

Do the two conflict?

No. They rest on the same fact. A lawyer who uses AI on work they have to sign still has to check it, and checking is work.

That is why the fee award survived. The court did not ask how fast the research was produced. It asked whether the hours actually spent were reasonable, and nobody showed that they were not.

It is also why the billing question stays open. If verification is the work, then the honest unit is the time spent verifying, not the time the tool would have taken a person. The difficulty is that verification is invisible. A reviewed answer and an unreviewed answer look the same on the page.

What does this mean for a law firm in practice?

Three things follow, none of them comfortable.

The defence in Denver won partly because it could say what it had done. It had checked every piece of AI assisted research, and the other side could not point at a single entry. A firm that cannot say the same is in a weaker position when a bill is challenged.

Disclosure now has a shape. AI software cost is overhead unless it is tied to a matter and explained to the client. That is a question for the finance team as much as for the lawyers.

And the hardest issue is the one neither source resolves: what to charge when the tool genuinely removes hours a client used to pay for. The New York report does not answer it. The Tenth Circuit was not asked.

Does this apply outside the United States?

The rulings do not, but the pressure does. The EU AI Act is in force. In Luxembourg, the CSSF’s 2026 annual report named hallucination, loss of human control and vendor lock-in among the risks facing the financial sector, and announced a survey of supervised firms on their use of AI. Law firms, fund management companies and fund administrators here will be asked what AI they use and how they control it, and an answer of “our people check everything” will be worth as much as the evidence behind it.

Frequently asked questions

Can a court reduce legal fees because a firm used AI?

Not automatically. In the case reported on 4 November 2026, the 10th US Circuit upheld a 115,000 dollar fee award, finding that the party challenging it had not shown the hours actually spent were unreasonable.

Can a law firm charge clients for AI software?

Under the New York State Bar Association report adopted in November 2026, the cost is overhead, and may be passed on as a disbursement only when it is tied to a specific matter and the basis for the charge is disclosed.

Do lawyers have to tell clients they used AI?

Per the same report, generally not for administrative uses such as word processing, spell checking, calendaring or timekeeping, as long as the use does not affect fees. Other uses are a different question.

Does AI remove the duty to check?

No. Both reports point the same way: the lawyer who signs remains responsible for verifying the authorities, the arguments and the answers.
