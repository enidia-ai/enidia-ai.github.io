Analysis · AI and legal fees

Should lawyers bill less when AI does the work?

The Enidia AI team
November 5, 2026

Two decisions from the same week pull in what looks like opposite directions. A federal appeals court refused to cut a fee award because the winning lawyers used AI. The New York State Bar Association told lawyers not to bill clients for time that AI saved them. Read together, they show where the value of a lawyer’s hour is moving.

The court: AI is not a reason to cut the bill

This week, the US Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit upheld a $115,000 fee award in a trade secrets case that Flux Drive, a maker of motor-efficiency equipment, had brought and lost. Flux Drive argued that the defence lawyers had billed for “ministerial tasks” and that their use of AI should have made the bill smaller.

The court disagreed. Writing for a unanimous panel, Judge Carolyn McHugh accepted that AI tools can make lawyers more efficient, but stressed that lawyers must still verify the authorities and arguments themselves. The defence said its lawyers had checked every piece of AI-assisted research, and Flux Drive could not point to a single inflated or redundant entry. In the court’s words: “Plaintiffs have not shown that defense counsel’s use of AI rendered the hours that they actually expended unreasonable.”

The defence partner, Christopher Ottele, put it simply: AI “is not a substitute for the judgment and analysis that attorneys still must exercise in every filing with the court.”

The bar: don’t bill for time you didn’t spend

In the same week, the New York State Bar Association adopted a report from its Committee on Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies. It calls for limits on billing clients for time saved by AI tools, or for time spent learning to use them. It treats the cost of AI software as overhead, which can be passed on only when it is tied to a specific matter and disclosed to the client. And it reminds supervising lawyers that a firm-approved tool does not remove the duty to review the work on each matter.

A contradiction, or two halves of one rule?

At first sight, a court protects lawyers who used AI from a fee cut, while a bar association tells them to pass the savings on. Look closer and both say the same thing: clients pay for the work a lawyer actually does, and checking is work.

  • If AI drafts in ten minutes what used to take five hours, those five hours were not spent and should not be billed.
  • If a lawyer then spends two hours reading the sources, testing the reasoning and fixing the draft, those two hours were spent, and a court will not strip them away because a machine wrote the first version.

The questions that remain are harder. If verification is what clients now pay for, what happens to the billable hour when tools get faster? How should a firm price a result that took one hour instead of ten, when the value to the client is the same? And who checks that the “verification time” on an invoice is real? Neither the court nor the bar answers these yet, and every firm using AI will have to.

What this means for law firms

The safe position today is clear. Keep records of what the AI produced and what your lawyers checked. Bill the checking, not the time the machine saved. Tell clients how you use AI where it affects fees. And make sure the review is real, because that is exactly what a court or a client will ask about.
